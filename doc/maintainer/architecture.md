# Architecture
How the single-player game is put together, for changing it without breaking determinism or feel.

## Layout
| Path | Holds |
|---|---|
| `src/core/` | The deterministic simulation: physics world, hand, stand, knife, cut, and the input wire format. Everything the server must agree on lives here |
| `src/wasm/` | `wasm-bindgen` bindings exposing the core and its layout constants to the browser |
| `src/server/` | `cake-server`, the multiplayer server: one game room per process, replaying each player's inputs in their own world to score them, and serving the client build. The build writes brotli and gzip copies beside each compressible file (`vite.config.ts`, cached by content in `<BUILD_DIR>/precompressed/`), which the server sends to a browser that accepts them; Vite's hashed `assets/` are cached for good, and every other file is revalidated by a tag of its content, read when the server starts. Its messages are in `protocol.md`. The `room-cost` example times its step with many players, some of them silent |
| `src/bake/` | `cake-bake`, a native tool that turns a repaired model into a `.cake` file with its collider hulls. See the asset pipeline in `tech-stack.md` |
| `tools/` | Setup and build helpers, and the model repair step `repair-model.py` with its tests |
| `src/client/` | Vite + Three.js client: drawing, mouse and keyboard handling, springs, camera, cut animation |
| `src/core/examples/` | `replay` prints determinism checkpoints for `make test`. `settle` prints how the cake moves through a scripted carry, drop, and stand turn, and `drops` how far and how long the cake moves after drops from many grab points, for tuning physics. Both take a `.cake` file as an argument in place of the placeholder. `knife_cost` times the tick with the knife pressed into given cakes, `throw_cost` while they are thrown about, and `new_game_cost` loading a round's cake by stage |

## One tick
The client eases raw mouse input through springs in JavaScript, quantizes the eased values, and sends them as one `Input` per 60 Hz tick. Easing before quantizing keeps JS math out of the simulation while letting every control feel smooth. The core then, in order:
1. Steers the hand toward the target, lifting the cake's bounding box just clear of the surface beneath it, or lets go the moment the hand input stops
2. Turns the stand toward the input angle, speed-limited, and a free cake resting on it by the same angle
3. Floats the knife's rest with the cake's height near the stand, or its middle height away from it, then sets its tilt and lowering, the lowering clamped where the curved edge meets the cake or the stand's plate, the tilt riding the edge up over small bumps
4. Slows a resting cake's spin, then steps Rapier, puts a free cake to sleep once its squash holds it or once it has stayed in place without speeding up for three quarters of a second, and respawns a cake that has been falling off the table for a few seconds

Every control is relative: a drag moves its object by how far the mouse travels from where it pressed, never toward the cursor, so no cursor path can flip an object. Springs only delay a drag's effect: a fast drag ends where a slow one of the same travel does, except on the stand, whose input is held within half a radian of the stand's actual angle so a fast circling drag cannot pile up turning the speed-limited stand would work off after release. The only clamp on a drag's target is a hard stop, where the core stopped the object short: the client sees it from the knife's tilt or lowering differing from what it sent, and from the stand's blocked flag, since the stand's speed limit already makes it lag its input. The knife's lowering is clamped only while the player presses it down, since a shortfall at any other time is the core riding the edge over a bump, and the edge should settle back once past it. Whatever a press would grab, or a drag is moving, is highlighted through a uniform its materials' shaders always carry (`highlight.ts`), whose strength each kind sets (the stand's is half), so turning it on or off compiles nothing; the page shows it only while its drags move the world, never once it has cut, while waiting for a start, or to a spectator.

## Knife and stand
The cut plane always passes through one fixed pivot, above the tallest any cake can stand on the stand in any pose. `CakeModel::validate` bounds every cake's extent so that holds. The knife rests below the pivot, slid down along its own blade to a few centimeters above the cake's current height on the stand. Away from the stand it waits over the middle of the cake's flattest and tallest heights instead, and turns to following the cake across `REST_APPROACH_M`, which reaches past where the cake spawns. No approach the table has room for lets the slow float rise clear of a cake carried in at its tallest at the hand's full speed, so such a carry can still meet the blade. The float is two equal eases in series because a single ease jumps into motion, while two in series still never pass their target. Sliding along the blade keeps the cut line in place as the rest floats, at the price of tilting swinging a low-resting edge in an arc about the high pivot. The player lowers the knife from its rest, so the input's lowering is relative to it. The knife has two axes, tilt about its fixed edge direction and lowering within its plane, and the stand turning the cake supplies the third, so knife and placement together still reach every plane. The mezzaluna's edge is a circular arc, so the lowering limit minimizes a convex function per section segment rather than taking the segment's endpoints.

The two sweeps check the cake's section up to about 36 times a tick while the knife is pressed in, which cost about 20 ms on a 40k-triangle cake when every check scanned every triangle. So each cake gets a triangle tree, a bounding volume hierarchy over the cake mesh in its own coordinates, built once in its `Cake` with everything else a world derives from the model alone (mass, collider shapes, width range). Every world on that cake shares the `Cake`, and the server builds each catalog cake once at startup, so a round's worlds build only their Rapier bodies. A check visits only boxes that straddle the plane and reach above the depth that could still change its answer, and a clearance check stops at the first segment that blocks. The tree skips only triangles that cannot change the answer, and gives a 1 micrometer margin to rounding, so every result matches the full scan to the bit. The stand's sweep turns the knife's plane into the cake's coordinates instead of turning a copy of every vertex, and turns only the vertices of the triangles it visits. `tests/knife_tree.rs` holds the full scan to that on real cakes.

A lowered knife blocks turns rather than rising out of their way. Tilting rides the edge up over a bump no higher than `MAX_RIDE_M` above the player's lowering, measured from the input rather than the knife's current lowering so successive bumps cannot ratchet it up a wall, and stops where it would have to rise further. Turning the stand under a cake resting on it stops where the edge would enter the cake, which also stops the cake's spin. Both sweeps stop a few milliradians short of contact. At contact a cake settling a hair would cross the blade's plane, where the lowering limit jumps from a wall's foot to its top, and the knife would leap up after all.

The knife collides only with a held cake. A free cake is never pushed by the blade, because the lowering limit already stops the edge at its surface, and a held cake is left out of that limit so the blade holds its place against it. A free cake that moves into the pressed blade by itself, tipping or sliding into its plane, would make that limit jump the knife onto it, so the world puts it back where it was before the step and stops it there, as the blade would, until the knife next moves. A thin Rapier collider for the blade's face was tried first: its contacts pushed a cake resting within a stopped turn's margin of the plane, and a face thin enough to stay clear of those margins let a tipping box through. A cake rising gently into the edge from below, under 5 mm a tick, raises the knife instead, and one crossing the plane above the blade's back still makes it jump. Contacts alone cannot stop a held cake: the kinematic hand's rigid grip outmuscles any contact and squeezes the cake through the thin blade. So each hand move first sweeps the cake's shape against the knife, stand, and table and stops or slides it there, which also keeps a carried cake from clipping into the stand, and the cake has CCD on so its swing cannot tunnel through either. Removing either one fails a core test. The sweep follows the grip's pull, from where the cake is held to the hand's next place, since a briskly carried cake lags the hand and the grip would otherwise drag it past where the hand stopped. A held cake also meets the space under the stand's plate as one solid cylinder, so a short cake carried along the table cannot slip in under the plate's overhang, where its hover target pins it against the plate's underside and the grip drags it along the column. A sweep alone could not stop that, because the cake's swing carries its far side on past the stopped grip.

A cut divides the whole cake along the plane wherever the blade's path reaches any of the section: within the blade's span and above where the blade stops on the stand or table. The section past the blade's ends or below the plate splits with it. A plane that crosses the cake only where the blade cannot reach is a miss that scores 0, and `Cut!` stays disabled while it would be one.

## Physics
Rapier's slop, contact prediction, and sleep thresholds scale with `length_unit`, which is set to a decimeter because the defaults assume meter-sized objects.

A convex hull face with many vertices resting on the table jitters in Rapier and never sleeps, even in a bare scene, and no solver setting fixes it. So collider hulls are built coarser than the drawn and weighed mesh, which also gives a cake on its side facets to stop on.

Damping cannot stop an off-balance cake rocking or rolling on its rim, because gravity keeps driving it. A soft cake squashing at its contact acts as rolling resistance instead: a fixed loss of spin per second, applied only while touching. It stops small rolls outright yet lets a cake tip over.

A rigid body rests only where its center of mass stands over what touches the surface, and most cakes are rounded, so a cake let go on its side rolls on to one of the few flat poses its shape has. Coarser hulls cannot add rests without sinking the drawn mesh into the table, since resting on a flat surface depends only on the hulls' convex hull, which matches the mesh's. A cake squashes instead: a slow free cake on the table or plate sleeps at once where its center of mass stands over the patch of its hulls within 2 mm of the surface, as if its hulls had a face cut that deep where they touch. It keeps no gravity while the squash holds it, since the stand's turn wakes it in the step and gravity would rock it off a pose only the squash holds; a grab, or anything that speeds it up, gives gravity back. The patch is wide on a gently curved side and narrow across a sharp edge, so a cake still tips off an edge unless set within a millimeter or so of balance. A cake leaning on the plate's rim has no flat patch and comes to rest by the stillness sleep below.

The stand's colliders are round, so the physics never turns them. Instead the world turns a free cake resting on the plate with the stand each tick, keeping its motion relative to the stand. A plate carrying a cake by friction walks it outward, about 20% of its distance from the axis per quarter turn, because Rapier's contacts match velocities, not positions: the cake moves along the tangent while the plate turns along the arc. Carrying it exactly also keeps the cake where the stand's sweep predicts, so a turn into a lowered blade stops at contact instead of the cake sliding on across the blade's plane.

Rapier sleeps a cake only once its farthest point stays under 5 mm/s for half a second. A cake rocking in place on a single contact, such as a face against the stand's rim, never does, since each step's gravity regains the spin the rolling resistance took off before it. So the world puts a free cake to sleep itself once every point has stayed within 5 mm of one pose for at least three quarters of a second, provided it moved no further in its last quarter second than in the one before. A cake tipping over from near balance creeps at first, since the rolling resistance takes off each tick's small spin, and could stay within 5 mm for that long, but it speeds up as it leans, which the comparison catches. The first quarter second is never compared, since the jolt of landing would make the creep after it look like slowing. Within a few milliradians of balance the creep stays under Rapier's own threshold and the cake rests balanced, as a soft cake squashed on its edge may.

A carried cake's lowest point clears the surface beneath it by a centimeter, the height a stand pickup lifts it. That surface eases from the table up to the stand's top along an S curve across most of the table, from 0.4 m out to the stand's rim, so a cake hanging low can still catch the rim and turn as it is dragged on. A cake grabbed while resting on the stand instead lifts only a centimeter above the grab point, so the plate still bears its far side and a gentle slide keeps its orientation, until its box leaves the stand and the usual carry takes over. Run the `settle` and `drops` examples before and after changing damping, friction, resistance, or speeds.

## Client
Performance beats power saving, but not for gains no player sees: the renderer asks for the high-performance GPU, and frames beyond the simulation's 60 Hz are not drawn. Development builds (`make run`) have no round time limit, and production builds use `ROUND_TICKS`. A page served by the server, or given `?server=<host:port>` in development, plays online (`net.ts`): it sends every tick's input, its `Cut!` tick, and a hash once a second, runs the server's rounds with the server's time limit, and between them covers the screen with the lobby or the results (`lobby.ts`). A spectator sees the round's cake untouched where the players' starts, the countdown, and the rules in a card over the empty top left, and steps nothing. The lobby asks for the name and standup preference before three.js, the scene, and the WASM load, unless the browser remembers both from input on the site within the hour (`profile.ts`): the page's entry, `startup.ts`, holds only the lobby, its dialog, sound, and the room's connection, which it opens at the join and whose messages it holds until the game has loaded, and loads the rest of the game behind the dialog. The game builds its scene and renderers only once the room names its first cake, after the join, so none of that holds a frame while the player types. The lobby lists the room's players and pick as they arrive, without waiting for the page's first cake to build, and builds the host's cake picker only when it first opens, so a page that never picks downloads no preview. An online page builds its first game on the first cake the room names, its pick or its round's, holding the room's messages until then, so it downloads no cake the room has no use for. A held `start` or `judging` counts its `elapsed_ms` from its arrival, so a late spectator's countdown and a late joiner's reveal keep the room's time. Every page in the lobby downloads and builds the pick the server announces, hidden, through the same stepped `Scene.loadCake` a round uses (`roundCakes.ts`), so the round on it swaps in without a load. Every round starts the camera from the same pose (`Orbit.resetForCake`), orbiting a point over the stand's axis at two thirds of the round's cake's height as served, standing on the plate; the judging aims at the same share of each judged cake's height. The view centers the table 12% of the canvas's height above its middle, through the same view offset as the opponents' shift, zoomed out by as much, so the cake's spawn clears the `Cut!` button's band and a drag moves as far as without the raise. A new round builds its game, its cake's geometry, and its texture, already uploaded to the GPU, one step per frame while the old round plays on, then swaps the game, controls, scene, and cut state in one synchronous step, so no frame mixes the two rounds. Every round's cake draws through the same meshes and materials, because three deletes a shader once no material uses it, and recompiling them every round cost a 100-180 ms frame. The cut animation is client-only: the simulation freezes and scores at the press, and the client then sweeps the knife down through the cake, splitting the halves apart by just the blade's thickness once the edge reaches the cake so the blade shows in the cut, rocks it with its curved edge rolling without slipping, lets it swing a little past center and settle. Online the halves then stay together until the judging reveal; offline the cut gets a short reveal of its own, the halves parting as their gram counters count up, then the score (`reveal.ts` holds the timing both share). Both halves slide from the count's start to its end, fast at first and slowing evenly to a stop with the larger half's counter, level, along the cut's normal flattened onto the table, so a tilted cut never sinks a half into the plate, and each ends the same fixed distance from where the knife cut it. Nothing limits the slide (owner's choice), so a half may hang past the plate's rim, hover, or pass into the stand. A weight label floats over each half's top, tied to it by a leader line that follows it as it slides (`halfLabels.ts`, through `Scene.showHalfGrams`, which the judging reveal can call every frame to count up). Each half's cut face is a plane drawn only where the half's surfaces, counted in the stencil buffer, show it is inside the cake, so it needs no cap geometry and works for any closed mesh. Its shader lays sponge layers, buttercream, and jam level in the cake model's own frame (`cutFace.ts`), so they stay as baked however the cake was set down, and box-filters each band by the pixel's footprint, so a band a few pixels wide stays crisp at a distance and a thinner one blends rather than shimmers. The shadow pass cannot use the stencil, so the plane casts no shadow; instead each half casts shadows from both its faces, which closes the gap where the cut faces away from the light. The sponge plane is centered on the cake rather than the pivot, since a cake cut away from the stand has a section far from the pivot.

During an online round, players and spectators alike see the other round players' tiles over the top right of their own full-screen view (`opponents.ts`), a page still loading the cake getting its tile only once it plays, drawn by a second WebGL renderer on its own transparent canvas laid over it, so the player's own scene, its camera, and its picking keep a canvas to themselves. The player's camera takes a view offset that centers the table on what the tiles leave uncovered (the uncovered area's centroid), which keeps picking right since the ray is cast through the same projection. One stand, knife, and cake, sharing the round's geometry and texture, are posed and drawn once per tile into a scissored viewport. Each tile plays its opponent's poses 4 ticks (two pose frames) behind the newest that arrived, interpolating between them and easing its playback rate toward that lag, so a late frame does not stall it and a stalled stream (a hidden tab) holds its last pose and then jumps ahead. An opponent's cut plays the same client-only knife motion as the player's own (`cutMotion.ts`), with its chop, once the tile's playback reaches the cut message's tick, from the message's pose, but leaves the cake whole, since the halves part only in the judging reveal and the blade's thickness is under a pixel at tile size. Once that motion ends, the tile is copied grayed into a 2D canvas of its own and never drawn again, so a finished opponent costs no draw calls; a layout change drops the copies and redraws them. The tiles' cake is built from the round's game a few frames after its swap and uploaded and compiled before it shows, so neither its build nor its first draw lands on the swap's long frames.

Once play ends, every page, players and spectators alike, shows the judging reveal (`judging.ts`) in the player's own scene, with the tiles closed and the scene's camera taken from the player: the server's `judging` message gives the order, and each scene is staged from that player's `cut` message on the round's one cake geometry, since a cut differs from another only in the cake's pose, the stand's angle, and the knife's plane (`Scene.stageCut`). What a scene needs that costs a pass over the cake's vertices, where its halves' labels point and the cake's bounds the camera frames, is found by `Scene.prepareCut` a 2 ms slice per frame from the moment each cut message arrives, during play and the 3 s before the judging, so a scene starts with nothing to compute. The camera's look target and distance are springs, so the cut from one cake to the next glides while the orbit's yaw carries on. A page that joins mid-reveal, or whose cake is still loading, finishes a missing preparation at once, and until the cake has loaded shows the judged player's name, and their score once counted, over the empty stand. A full-screen overlay takes every press, so no one drags the camera or the cake during it. When it ends, the scene shows the page's own cake whole again, since a page that next spectates a round on the same cake keeps its untouched game and scene rather than reloading them.
