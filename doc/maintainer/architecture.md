# Architecture
How the single-player game is put together, for changing it without breaking determinism or feel.

## Layout
| Path | Holds |
|---|---|
| `src/core/` | The deterministic simulation: physics world, hand, stand, knife, cut, and the input wire format. Everything the server must agree on lives here |
| `src/wasm/` | `wasm-bindgen` bindings exposing the core and its layout constants to the browser |
| `src/server/` | `cake-server`, the multiplayer server: one game room per process, replaying each player's inputs in their own world to score them, and serving the client build. Its messages are in `protocol.md`. The `room-cost` example times its step with many players |
| `src/bake/` | `cake-bake`, a native tool that turns a repaired model into a `.cake` file with its collider hulls. See the asset pipeline in `tech-stack.md` |
| `tools/` | Setup and build helpers, and the model repair step `repair-model.py` with its tests |
| `src/client/` | Vite + Three.js client: drawing, mouse and keyboard handling, springs, camera, cut animation |
| `src/core/examples/` | `replay` prints determinism checkpoints for `make test`. `settle` prints how the cake moves through a scripted carry, drop, and stand turn, and `drops` how far and how long the cake moves after drops from many grab points, for tuning physics. Both take a `.cake` file as an argument in place of the placeholder. `knife_cost` times the tick with the knife pressed into given cakes, and `new_game_cost` loading a round's cake by stage |

## One tick
The client eases raw mouse input through springs in JavaScript, quantizes the eased values, and sends them as one `Input` per 60 Hz tick. Easing before quantizing keeps JS math out of the simulation while letting every control feel smooth. The core then, in order:
1. Steers the hand toward the target, lifting the cake's bounding box just clear of the surface beneath it, or lets go the moment the hand input stops
2. Turns the stand toward the input angle, speed-limited
3. Floats the knife's rest with the cake's height near the stand, or its middle height away from it, then sets its tilt and lowering, the lowering clamped where the curved edge meets the cake or the stand's plate, the tilt riding the edge up over small bumps
4. Slows a resting cake's spin relative to its support, then steps Rapier and respawns a cake that has been falling off the table for a few seconds

Every control is relative: a drag moves its object by how far the mouse travels from where it pressed, never toward the cursor, so no cursor path can flip an object. Springs only delay a drag's effect: a fast drag ends where a slow one of the same travel does, except on the stand, whose input is held within half a radian of the stand's actual angle so a fast circling drag cannot pile up turning the speed-limited stand would work off after release. The only clamp on a drag's target is a hard stop, where the core stopped the object short: the client sees it from the knife's tilt or lowering differing from what it sent, and from the stand's blocked flag, since the stand's speed limit already makes it lag its input. The knife's lowering is clamped only while the player presses it down, since a shortfall at any other time is the core riding the edge over a bump, and the edge should settle back once past it.

## Knife and stand
The cut plane always passes through one fixed pivot, above the tallest any cake can stand on the stand in any pose. `CakeModel::validate` bounds every cake's extent so that holds. The knife rests below the pivot, slid down along its own blade to a few centimeters above the cake's current height on the stand. Away from the stand it waits over the middle of the cake's flattest and tallest heights instead, and turns to following the cake across `REST_APPROACH_M`, which reaches past where the cake spawns. No approach the table has room for lets the slow float rise clear of a cake carried in at its tallest at the hand's full speed, so such a carry can still meet the blade. The float is two equal eases in series because a single ease jumps into motion, while two in series still never pass their target. Sliding along the blade keeps the cut line in place as the rest floats, at the price of tilting swinging a low-resting edge in an arc about the high pivot. The player lowers the knife from its rest, so the input's lowering is relative to it. The knife has two axes, tilt about its fixed edge direction and lowering within its plane, and the stand turning the cake supplies the third, so knife and placement together still reach every plane. The mezzaluna's edge is a circular arc, so the lowering limit minimizes a convex function per section segment rather than taking the segment's endpoints.

The two sweeps check the cake's section up to about 36 times a tick while the knife is pressed in, which cost about 20 ms on a 40k-triangle cake when every check scanned every triangle. So each cake gets a triangle tree, a bounding volume hierarchy over the cake mesh in its own coordinates, built once in its `Cake` with everything else a world derives from the model alone (mass, collider shapes, width range). Every world on that cake shares the `Cake`, and the server builds each catalog cake once at startup, so a round's worlds build only their Rapier bodies. A check visits only boxes that straddle the plane and reach above the depth that could still change its answer, and a clearance check stops at the first segment that blocks. The tree skips only triangles that cannot change the answer, and gives a 1 micrometer margin to rounding, so every result matches the full scan to the bit. The stand's sweep turns the knife's plane into the cake's coordinates instead of turning a copy of every vertex, and turns only the vertices of the triangles it visits. `tests/knife_tree.rs` holds the full scan to that on real cakes.

A lowered knife blocks turns rather than rising out of their way. Tilting rides the edge up over a bump no higher than `MAX_RIDE_M` above the player's lowering, measured from the input rather than the knife's current lowering so successive bumps cannot ratchet it up a wall, and stops where it would have to rise further. Turning the stand under a cake resting on it stops where the edge would enter the cake, which also stops the cake's spin. Both sweeps stop a few milliradians short of contact. At contact a cake settling a hair would cross the blade's plane, where the lowering limit jumps from a wall's foot to its top, and the knife would leap up after all.

The knife collides only with a held cake. A free cake is never pushed by the blade, because the lowering limit already stops the edge at its surface, and a held cake is left out of that limit so the blade holds its place against it. Contacts alone cannot stop a held cake: the kinematic hand's rigid grip outmuscles any contact and squeezes the cake through the thin blade. So each hand move first sweeps the cake's shape against the knife, stand, and table and stops or slides it there, which also keeps a carried cake from clipping into the stand, and the cake has CCD on so its swing cannot tunnel through either. Removing either one fails a core test.

A cut divides the whole cake along the plane wherever the blade's path reaches any of the section: within the blade's span and above where the blade stops on the stand or table. The section past the blade's ends or below the plate splits with it. A plane that crosses the cake only where the blade cannot reach is a miss that scores 0, and `Cut!` stays disabled while it would be one.

## Physics
Rapier's slop, contact prediction, and sleep thresholds scale with `length_unit`, which is set to a decimeter because the defaults assume meter-sized objects.

A convex hull face with many vertices resting on the table jitters in Rapier and never sleeps, even in a bare scene, and no solver setting fixes it. So collider hulls are built coarser than the drawn and weighed mesh, which also gives a cake on its side facets to stop on.

Damping cannot stop an off-balance cake rocking or rolling on its rim, because gravity keeps driving it. A soft cake squashing at its contact acts as rolling resistance instead: a fixed loss of spin per second, relative to the table or stand beneath, applied only while touching. It stops small rolls outright yet lets a cake tip over, and being relative to the support keeps the turning stand carrying the cake.

A carried cake's lowest point clears the surface beneath it by a centimeter, the height a stand pickup lifts it. That surface eases from the table up to the stand's top along an S curve across most of the table, from 0.4 m out to the stand's rim, so a cake hanging low can still catch the rim and turn as it is dragged on. A cake grabbed while resting on the stand instead lifts only a centimeter above the grab point, so the plate still bears its far side and a gentle slide keeps its orientation, until its box leaves the stand and the usual carry takes over. Run the `settle` and `drops` examples before and after changing damping, friction, resistance, or speeds.

## Client
Performance beats power saving, but not for gains no player sees: the renderer asks for the high-performance GPU, and frames beyond the simulation's 60 Hz are not drawn. Development builds (`make run`) have no round time limit, and production builds use `ROUND_TICKS`. A page served by the server, or given `?server=<host:port>` in development, plays online (`net.ts`): it sends every tick's input, its `Cut!` tick, and a hash once a second, runs the server's rounds with the server's time limit, and between them lets the player practice on a random cake with no limit, under the lobby (`lobby.ts`). The lobby asks for the name before the WASM loads, and builds the host's cake picker only once the page hosts, so a page that never hosts downloads no preview. A new round loads its cake and decodes its texture while the old round plays on, then swaps the game, controls, scene, and cut state in one synchronous step, so no frame mixes the two rounds. The cut animation is client-only: the simulation freezes and scores at the press, and the client then sweeps the knife down through the cake, splitting the halves apart by just the blade's thickness once the edge reaches the cake so the blade shows in the cut, rocks it with its curved edge rolling without slipping, lets it swing a little past center and settle, and parts the halves. They slide level, along the cut's normal flattened onto the table, so a tilted cut never sinks one half into the plate, and a weight label floats over each half's top, tied to it by a leader line that follows it as it slides (`halfLabels.ts`, through `Scene.showHalfGrams`, which the judging reveal can call every frame to count up). Each half's cut face is a sponge plane drawn only where the half's surfaces, counted in the stencil buffer, show it is inside the cake, so it needs no cap geometry and works for any closed mesh. The sponge plane is centered on the cake rather than the pivot, since a cake cut away from the stand has a section far from the pivot.

During an online round, players and spectators alike see the other round players' tiles on the right (`opponents.ts`), drawn by a second WebGL renderer on its own canvas, so the player's own scene, its camera, and its picking keep a canvas to themselves. One stand, knife, and cake, sharing the round's geometry and texture, are posed and drawn once per tile into a scissored viewport. Each tile plays its opponent's poses 4 ticks (two pose frames) behind the newest that arrived, interpolating between them and easing its playback rate toward that lag, so a late frame does not stall it and a stalled stream (a hidden tab) holds its last pose and then jumps ahead. An opponent's cut plays the same client-only knife motion as the player's own (`cutMotion.ts`), from the cut message's pose, but leaves the cake whole, since the halves part only in the judging reveal and the blade's thickness is under a pixel at tile size. The tiles' cake is built from the round's game a few frames after its swap and uploaded and compiled before it shows, so neither its build nor its first draw lands on the swap's long frames.
